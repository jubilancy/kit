## 4. blog post

```html
<!--# layout file="~/layouts/main.html" -->

<!--# block name="description" -->thoughts on personal sites and why they matter<!--# endblock -->

<div class="page-eyebrow">blog · 2026-05-30</div>
<h1>on the small web and slowness</h1>
<p class="page-meta">
  written <!--# flastmod file="~/blog/posts/on-small-web.html" fmt="%A, %B %dd %Y" timezone="America/New_York" -->
  &nbsp;·&nbsp; size: <!--# fsize file="~/blog/posts/on-small-web.html" unit="kb" --> KB
</p>

<p>
  the term "small web" keeps coming up in conversations about personal sites.
  i like it not because it implies low traffic but because it implies scale —
  things sized for a human to make and maintain, without a team, without a roadmap.
</p>

<p>
  a personal site exists on a different timeline than a product. it doesn't have
  a launch. it just... is, and then gets added to, and sometimes sits untouched
  for months, and that's fine. the not-updating is as honest as the updating.
</p>

<h2>why slowness matters</h2>

<p>
  the web got fast and it got worse. pages load in milliseconds and feel hollow.
  something about the texture of a site you can tell a person made — the slightly
  inconsistent padding, the font choice that reveals something about them — makes
  it worth reading even if the prose is modest.
</p>

<p>
  i'm not being nostalgic for dial-up. i'm being nostalgic for intention.
  most of what's on the web now exists because an algorithm or an incentive
  made it exist. personal sites exist because someone decided they wanted one.
  that decision is the content, in a way.
</p>
```
